Sama²

Sama² is a shared organiser for Malaysian families, built at Nasi Forge PLT on Rails 8 and Bullet Train. It lives at sama2.my. Around the organiser there is a games hub, and a set of Bento-style public profiles that a person can hang on their own domain.

The part worth writing up is not a feature. In late June 2026 I planned a whole visual identity for the app, neumorphic, soft, pillowy, the kind of thing that looks lovely in a screenshot. Seven weeks later I deleted it and shipped a different design system in its place, called Sama² Paper. The reason was that neumorphism was actively bad for the people Sama² is for. The interesting part is what the reversal cost, which was not what I expected, and what it taught me about where a visual language actually lives in a codebase.

01 · who it's for

What Sama² Is, and Who It's For

Three surfaces, and they do not behave the same way.

The first is the logged-in app: the organiser proper, where a family keeps its tasks, its money, its bookings. This is the thing people sign in to on a Tuesday night. The second is a games hub. The third is the Bento profile, a public page you can put on your own domain, which is the one part of the design I have never once wanted to change.

Who it is for matters more than any of that, and it is the sentence every decision below hangs off. Sama² is built for people who are not digital natives. Low end of digital adoption. The parent who has one app they trust and did not choose it. The relative who taps something, nothing visibly happens, and quietly concludes they broke it. Not the user you design for when you are picking a design language off a mood board.

If you hold that audience in your head, a lot of aesthetic questions stop being questions. Can this person see the edge of the button? Can they tell the button apart from the card it sits on? Is the tap target big enough for a thumb that is not steady? Those are the only three questions that ever really mattered here, and it still took me two design systems to start answering them properly.

02 · inherit, delete

The First Year: Inherit, Patch, Delete

A caveat before any number, because otherwise the numbers lie.

The repository reports 4,054 commits and a first commit dated 15 November 2016, authored by Andrew Culver. That is Bullet Train's own history, inherited whole when I started from the framework. It is not Sama². Sama²'s first commit is 7edff0f5, 13 August 2025, and its subject line is "Run configuration script." Since that commit there have been 1,759, of which 1,610 are mine and 149 were authored by an agent. The repo looks ten years old and it is one.

The inheritance is not just a footnote on a commit graph, it is the reason the frontend of this app was never really designed. Tailwind was adopted upstream in 2020. Turbolinks was replaced by Turbo in 2021. Webpacker was replaced by esbuild in 2022. Every one of those was settled years before Sama² existed. I made no greenfield frontend decisions at all. The frontend arrived already decided, and the only moves available to me were on top of it.

So the first year has a rhythm to it: build something bespoke, live with it, then delete it in favour of what the framework already did. I built a bespoke mobile navigation on 21 September 2025 and deleted it on 27 December 2025, going back to the Bullet Train default. I wrote a six-step wizard, 513 lines of it, and on 25 May 2026 replaced the whole thing with a single-sheet form. May 2026 is also the busiest month in the repo's history at 595 commits, which tells you roughly what that period felt like.

The one visual idea that survives all of it is the Bento profile. It has gone through every later redesign untouched. I do not have a tidy theory for why that one held and the rest did not, beyond the obvious: it was the only piece I designed for a specific person looking at a specific page, rather than for the app as a system.

03 · the argument

Neumorphism, and the Argument That Killed It

The neumorphic layer was the first whole-app identity I planned before building it. There was a Phase 0 plan document and a specialist review. Plan commit 27 June 2026, deletion merge 15 August 2026: 49 days, exactly seven weeks. (The idea itself had landed earlier, on 27 April 2026, so end to end the neumorphic look lived 110 days. The seven weeks is the formal programme.)

Here is the tell I should have read at the time. From the very beginning, the neumorphic implementation kept a hairline border on every surface. Pure neumorphism does not have borders. That is the whole point of it, shape emerges from shadow alone. I put borders on anyway, on day one, because without them nothing had enough contrast to be legible. I had already diagnosed the problem. I just filed it as a tweak instead of as a verdict.

The verdict got written down properly in the Paper design docs, blunter than I would have put it about my own work:

Pure neumorphism relies on low-contrast, blurred, same-color-on-same-color shadows and removes clear edges — which is directly hostile to low-vision and low-confidence users.

Directly hostile. To exactly the users Sama² exists for. Once that sentence is on the page there is nothing left to argue about.

Same design, not the same bytes

A note on the before-and-after images above: they are a reconstruction, not an archive. Both were captured at the same commit by flipping a cookie to switch skins, which means the "before" shots render 48px touch targets where the neumorphic era actually shipped 44px, and self-hosted Inter where the era pulled Inter from a CDN. Same design, not the same bytes. I would rather say that here than let you assume these are period screenshots.

There is a second finding from that era that I keep turning over, because it says something about how design systems actually fail. The neumorphic design document specified three typefaces: DM Sans for UI, Nunito for display, JetBrains Mono for mono. JetBrains Mono did ship, on 11 July 2026, for small mono labels. DM Sans and Nunito never loaded a single byte. Not once, not anywhere. neumorphism.css declares zero font-family rules. The Tailwind config overrides mono and only mono, so sans quietly stayed whatever the theme gem said it was, which was Inter. And for the entire life of the layer, 27 June to 13 August 2026, that Inter was being pulled from rsms.me, a CDN belonging to someone else, until I self-hosted it right at the end.

So the era had a documented typographic identity it never rendered, and an undocumented one it rendered on every page, fetched from a domain I do not own. Nobody noticed because nobody was looking at the fonts. Everybody was looking at the shadows.

04 · one line back

Paper, and Why the Reversal Was Cheap

Sama² Paper makes three choices, and all three fall straight out of who it's for.

Edges do the structural work. Where neumorphism asked a blurred shadow to imply where a card ends, Paper draws the line. If you need to know where the button is, the button has an outline. That is not a style preference, it is the correction.

The typeface is Atkinson Hyperlegible, designed by the Braille Institute specifically so that characters that usually blur into each other stay distinguishable for low-vision readers. It is self-hosted, and the reason is worth stating because it is not aesthetic: the service worker skips cross-origin requests, so a font served from a CDN could never enter the offline cache. A CDN font is a font that vanishes the moment the connection does. Given the audience, that was not a trade I was willing to make twice.

And every tappable target has a floor of 48px. Thumbs, not cursors.

The reason I could throw away seven weeks of work without it being a disaster is the mechanism underneath. Every design language in the app is a skin, scoped under a data-skin attribute on <body>. That means switching the app's entire look is a declaration, and rolling it back is the same declaration with a different string. One default_skin line on the account application controller covers all 65 controllers under it, so the whole logged-in app got reskinned by editing one line. The public marketing pages, all four games, the Bento profiles and the Devise auth screens are deliberately carved out and stay undeclared.

There was one more lever like that. Changing a single wrapper class on the ejected scaffold-box partial reskinned about 69 index and show pages with zero per-view edits, because nearly every scaffolded resource in a Bullet Train app renders through that one box.

And now the honest part, which is the thing I actually want anyone reading this to take away. Replacing the visual design was roughly 10% of the work. The neumorphic classes were the visible tip. The real visual language of the app was never in a stylesheet at all. It was approximately 7,700 Tailwind colour utilities spread across 625 ERB files. That is what a design language looks like when it lives in your markup instead of in your tokens, and it is why "just change the theme" was a sentence I had no business saying out loud.

05 · two looks

Where It Stands

It is not finished, and pretending otherwise would be the wrong ending.

The app genuinely has two looks right now. The games and the Bento profiles are still un-skinned, on purpose, but on purpose is not the same as resolved. Move between the organiser and a game and you can see the seam.

The typography has a gap I have not closed either. Sama² ships five locales, and the file counts show what that really means: 70 files for en, 39 for ms, 26 for en-MY, 5 for zh-CN, 5 for ta. Atkinson Hyperlegible is Latin-only. So a Chinese or Tamil reader falls back to the system stack and gets none of the character-disambiguation reasoning that made me choose the typeface. The argument that killed neumorphism does not yet reach every reader in the app.

And there is a smaller thing that I like, because it is the kind of conflict you only find by shipping. An old touch_optimizations.css pins touch targets at 44px. Paper's floor is 48px. Two rules, both written by me, both defensible, disagreeing about how big a button should be.

That last one is the whole project in miniature. You do not discover that a design system is hostile to your users by looking at it. You discover it by building it, shipping it, and then reading your own hairline borders back to yourself as evidence.
